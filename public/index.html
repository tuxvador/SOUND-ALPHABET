How a word becomes one sound

A Dorian  /  90 characters × 2 lengths

Every tone is a real note of one scale — A Dorian, from C4 up to A7. Nothing lands between pitches, so whatever letters you happen to sound together stack into a chord that belongs to a key rather than a cluster of arbitrary frequencies.

A letter is a pair of notes, and the interval between them is what identifies it. Ten notes yield exactly forty-five pairs — one per character, with none left over: twenty-six letters, ten digits, and nine punctuation marks. Each position in the word owns its own ten-note register, stacked low to high, so the first letter sings in the bottom octave, the second above it, the third above that. Because the registers do not overlap, two letters can never claim the same note: the reading stays unambiguous and anagrams still sound different.

The scale is deliberately gapped. Its notes sit at least 22 Hz apart — nearly four analysis bins — which is what lets the receiver separate them at all. A denser scale would sound richer and decode worse; this is the trade that keeps the chord both musical and readable.

Length is a second dimension. Notes are a frequency budget, and forty-five pairs is all a ten-note register holds. But how long a chord rings costs no notes at all — so the same forty-five pairs mean one thing in a chord of one beat and something else entirely in a chord of two. That doubles the alphabet to ninety without widening a register: letters, digits and common punctuation stay short, while rarer symbols like ; = € ¿ ride in the long set and take twice as long to sound. The receiver never assumes your tempo — it compares each chord against the shortest it has heard, so any BPM decodes.

Accents get no pairs of their own — all forty-five are spoken for. Instead an accented letter splits into its plain base and its mark: É is E plus an acute. The base uses its usual pair, and the mark is carried by a third note from the same register, sounded at 45% level. Since power falls with the square of amplitude, that note lands at about a fifth of the pair's — unmistakably present, yet never loud enough to be mistaken for part of the letter. Seven marks cover French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, German and Swedish; ligatures like Œ and ß spell themselves out as OE and SS.

Test signals

Decode a generated signal without a microphone. The audio is built in the page and fed straight to the receiver, so nothing depends on your speakers, your room, or microphone permission. Play it aloud instead to test the acoustic path.

Play the transmitter aloud through speakers with this listening — the message crosses the room as sound and reassembles itself. The receiver runs twelve Goertzel filters, one per frequency in the two banks. Each reports the energy at exactly its own frequency, which is far cheaper than a full spectrum when you already know the only twelve places a tone can hide. The strongest row and the strongest column must both clear the noise floor by a set margin before a character is accepted, so room noise alone never spells anything.
